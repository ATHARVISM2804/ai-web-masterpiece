# Bali culture research for the Canggu world bible

Date: 2026-11-12
Purpose: reference for depicting Balinese local life in a compressed, hand-built Canggu accurately and respectfully.

How to read this file:
- Facts with no tag were confirmed against at least one source listed at the end during this research pass.
- **(unverified)** = widely repeated or from general background knowledge, but not confirmed against a source in this pass. Check with a Balinese reviewer before it becomes on-screen text.
- **(sources differ)** = reliable sources disagree; the disagreement is described.
- Practice varies by village (the Balinese phrase is *desa, kala, patra*: place, time, circumstance (unverified wording)). Nothing here is a single island-wide rule. The local banjar / desa adat is the authority.
- Biggest gap: almost nothing village-specific to Canggu (banjar names, subak names, local odalan dates) could be found in English sources. See "Open questions" near the end.

## 1. Daily religious life (Balinese Hinduism)

### Framing
- The religion is Agama Hindu Dharma, practised by most Balinese; it is a daily, household-level practice, not only a temple one.
- Guiding idea: **Tri Hita Karana**, the three sources of wellbeing: harmony with the divine, with other people, and with nature. UNESCO uses this exact philosophy as the basis of the subak World Heritage listing.
- Second key idea: **Rwa Bhineda**, paired opposites (light/dark, good/bad) that only have meaning together and must be kept in balance, not defeated. This is why offerings go both up (to gods) and down (to lower spirits).
- Orientation matters: **kaja** (toward the mountain, sacred) and **kelod** (toward the sea). Temples and house compounds are laid out on this axis. In Canggu (south coast), kaja is roughly north/inland and kelod is the beach.

### Canang sari (the daily offering)
- A small square or round tray of woven young coconut leaf (**ceper**, made of *janur*), which stands for the earth / physical body.
- Inside:
  - **Porosan**: a small bundle of betel leaf, lime (chalk) and gambier/areca, honouring the Trimurti (Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva) and also read as thought, speech and action. Which ingredient maps to which god **(sources differ)**.
  - **Rice** grains, symbolising the life force (atma).
  - **Sampian urasari**: a palm-leaf ornament representing the wheel of life.
  - **Flowers by direction and colour** (the most common scheme): white to the east (Iswara), red to the south (Brahma), yellow to the west (Mahadeva), blue or green to the north (Vishnu). Some accounts say the layout is not strictly directional **(sources differ)**.
  - Shredded pandan leaf on top (*kembang rampai*) **(unverified)**.
  - Often a small extra on top: a coin or small banknote, a sweet, a cracker, a cigarette (*sesari*, the "essence" of the gift) **(unverified)**.
- "Canang" is the offering, "sari" is essence. It is a thank-you, not a request **(unverified wording, commonly given)**.
- Completed with a lit **incense stick (dupa)** and a sprinkle or flick of **holy water (tirta)** using a flower, with a short prayer. The smoke carries the essence upward **(unverified)**.
- **Who makes them**: mostly the women of the household, as daily devotional work; busy households and shops also buy them ready-made at the morning market or from roadside sellers **(unverified, widely reported)**.
- **Where**: house shrine, family temple, the house gate, in front of shops and warungs, on shop counters and cash registers, on motorbike seats / car dashboards, at crossroads, at big trees and statues, on the beach, at rice-field shrines.
- **When**: every day, mostly in the morning after cooking and before the household eats; some households also place them in late afternoon. More and larger offerings on Purnama, Tilem, Kajeng Kliwon and holy days **(timing partly unverified)**.
- Once the incense has burned out, the essence has been received. The physical remains are swept up later; dogs, chickens and birds eating the rice afterwards is normal and not an offence **(unverified)**.
- **Etiquette**: do not step on or over one deliberately, do not kick it, do not move it, do not pick it up. Stepping on, damaging or interfering with offerings is on the official Bali Province list of prohibitions for visitors. Accidentally brushing a spent one on a crowded pavement is understood; walk around when you can.

### Segehan (ground offerings)
- Small offerings on banana-leaf squares placed **on the ground** at thresholds and crossroads. They belong to *Bhuta Yadnya*, offerings to the lower spirits (bhuta kala), to keep them satisfied and the balance intact.
- Game-relevant distinction: **raised shrine = for gods and ancestors; ground = for lower spirits.** Both are sacred. Do not treat the ground ones as litter.

### House and family temples
- A traditional house is a **walled compound** of separate pavilions around an open courtyard (**natah**), not one building.
- The **family temple (sanggah, or merajan in higher-status families)** sits in the **kaja-kangin corner** (mountainward-east, i.e. northeast in south Bali), the most sacred part of the compound. Its core shrine is the **sanggah kemulan**, for the deified ancestors.
- Compound entrance is a single narrow roofed gate, the **angkul-angkul**, usually taller than the wall. Directly behind it is the **aling-aling**, a short screen wall (see section 5).
- Other pavilions (naming and use **sources differ**): **bale dangin** (east, ceremonies and rites of passage), **bale dauh** (west, daily life and guests), **bale daja / meten** (north, sleeping pavilion of the household heads), **paon** (kitchen, to the south, linked to Brahma/fire).
- Small shrine near the gate or in the yard for the guardian of the land (*penunggun karang*) **(unverified)**.
- Layout follows traditional building rules (**Asta Kosala Kosali**), with measurements taken from the owner's own body **(unverified)**.
- Villas and shops built for foreigners usually still have at least one small shrine, and staff place offerings there daily **(unverified, widely observed)**.

### Village temples (Kahyangan Tiga)
Every traditional village (desa adat) has three core temples:
- **Pura Puseh**: temple of origin, honours Vishnu; toward the mountain (kaja) end.
- **Pura Desa (Bale Agung)**: village temple, honours Brahma; at the village centre, used for community ritual and meetings.
- **Pura Dalem**: honours Shiva / Durga; at the seaward (kelod) end, usually beside the cemetery and cremation ground. The most solemn of the three. Do not design gameplay around it.

Other temple types a Canggu resident sees:
- **Pura Segara**: sea temples honouring sea deities; where melasti processions end.
- **Pura Subak / Bedugul / Ulun Suwi**: small rice-field and water temples belonging to the irrigation cooperative.
- **Pura Tirta**: water temples whose priests help coordinate irrigation.
- **Sad Kahyangan / Kahyangan Jagat**: the island's great directional temples (Tanah Lot is the famous sea temple a short drive from Canggu).
- Family and clan temples, market temples (*pura melanting*) **(unverified)**.

### Temple layout (three zones)
- **Nista mandala / jaba pisan**: outer zone, often an open yard for preparation and festival overflow.
- **Madya mandala / jaba tengah**: middle zone with the kulkul tower, gamelan pavilion, meeting pavilion (wantilan), kitchen.
- **Utama mandala / jeroan**: innermost and holiest, with the **padmasana** (empty lotus throne for the supreme god) and **meru** (tiered thatched towers, always an odd number of roofs **(unverified)**).
- Official rule for visitors: non-worshippers may not enter the utama and madya mandala; entry is for people who are praying, in Balinese traditional dress. See section 4.

### Canggu's own sea temples
- **Pura Batu Bolong** and **Pura Batu Mejan** are the two main coastal temples; both are described as centuries old and attributed to the 16th-century priest **Dang Hyang Nirartha**, who is also linked to Tanah Lot and Uluwatu.
- Whether Batu Bolong and Batu Mejan are two sites or one complex with two names **(sources differ)**. Wikipedia treats them as two; Echo Beach is also called Batu Mejan beach.
- Pura Batu Bolong is said to honour Ida Batara Segara (the sea deity) **(single travel source)**.
- Game implication: the temple stands right behind a surf beach. Surfers, beach bars and ceremonies share the same sand. That contrast is real and worth showing.

### Morning versus evening: what a resident sees
Morning (roughly 6:00 to 9:00):
- Women in sarong and sash walking the compound and shopfront with a tray of canang, a flower, a small bowl of holy water and smoking incense.
- Fresh bright offerings on every doorstep, counter and scooter; thin incense smoke at ankle height.
- Morning market busy with flower, janur and ready-made offering sellers.
- Small rice-on-banana-leaf offerings (*banten saiban*) set out after cooking **(unverified)**.

Evening (around sunset):
- Second round of offerings at some homes; incense again.
- **Tri Sandhya** prayer three times a day (about 6:00, 12:00, 18:00); the broadcast version is heard from banjar loudspeakers and local TV/radio **(times unverified)**.
- On Purnama (full moon) and Tilem (new moon) evenings: families in temple dress riding to the village temple with offering baskets.
- Gamelan practice drifting out of the bale banjar **(unverified, widely observed)**.
- Spent canang from the morning, flattened and faded, swept up with a palm-rib broom (*sapu lidi*).

## 2. Ceremonies and the calendar a resident notices

### Two calendars run at once
- **Pawukon**: a 210-day cycle. Galungan, Kuningan and most temple anniversaries (odalan) follow it, so they come round roughly every seven months.
- **Saka**: a lunar calendar. Nyepi, Purnama and Tilem follow it.
- Result: there is nearly always a ceremony somewhere nearby. A resident's week is regularly shaped by one.

### Galungan and Kuningan
- **Meaning**: Galungan marks the victory of dharma over adharma; ancestral spirits visit the family home for the ten days until Kuningan. Whether spirits "leave" or "return to heaven" on Kuningan is worded differently by source; the shared meaning is a farewell.
- **Lead-up days**:
  - **Penyekeban** (3 days before): green bananas covered in clay pots to ripen.
  - **Penyajaan** (2 days before): making **jaja**, coloured rice-dough cakes, for offerings.
  - **Penampahan** (1 day before): pigs or chickens slaughtered; lawar and sate made; penjor go up.
  - **Galungan** (always a Wednesday **(unverified)**): everyone in temple dress, family temple then village temples, streets lined with penjor.
  - **Manis Galungan** (day after): visiting relatives, outings; roads and beaches busy with local families.
  - **Kuningan** (10 days after Galungan): farewell offerings, including yellow rice; prayers are finished by midday **(yellow rice and the noon detail unverified)**.
- **Dates**: 17 June 2026 (Kuningan 27 June 2026); 13 January 2027 (Kuningan 23 January); 11 August 2027 (Kuningan 21 August). 2026 had only one Galungan because of where the 210-day cycle fell.
- **What is affected**: many small businesses close or run short hours; staff go home to their village.

### Penjor
- A tall bamboo pole, curved at the tip, dressed with young coconut leaf, with rice, coconut, fruit, cakes and cloth tied on. Planted on the right side of each house gate for Galungan **(side unverified)**.
- **Meaning**: a symbol of prosperity and gratitude. The arch is read as **Mount Agung**, the sacred mountain; another reading sees the pole as the dragon **Naga Basuki** (water, prosperity, safety). The hanging produce stands for the fruits of the earth.
- At the foot is a small bamboo shrine (**sanggah penjor**) where the family places Galungan offerings.
- The tip carries a hanging woven ornament (*sampian penjor*) **(unverified)**.
- Stays up about 35 days after Galungan before being taken down **(unverified)**.
- A plainer decorative penjor is also used for weddings and temple festivals; the religious Galungan penjor has required elements **(unverified)**.

### Nyepi (Day of Silence, Saka New Year)
- **Dates**: 19 March 2026. For 2027, Wikipedia lists 9 March, several travel calendars list 8 March **(sources differ; confirm against the official Indonesian holiday decree)**. 2028: 26 March.
- **Before**:
  - **Melasti**, 3 to 4 days before: village processions carry sacred temple objects to the sea for purification and collect holy water.
  - **Tawur Kesanga**, the eve: large offerings at crossroads.
  - **Pengrupukan**, at sunset on the eve: noise-making with pots, bamboo and coconut-leaf torches to drive out negative spirits, then the **ogoh-ogoh** parade.
- **Ogoh-ogoh**: giant figures of bamboo, papier-mache, cloth and foam, representing bhuta kala (negative forces) or mythological demons. Built over weeks by each banjar's youth group (*sekaa teruna* **(unverified term)**), carried on bamboo grids by teams, spun at crossroads to confuse the spirits **(spinning unverified)**, then traditionally burned at the cemetery. Some are kept or sold.
- **Nyepi itself**, 6:00 to 6:00 the next morning. The four abstentions (*Catur Brata Penyepian*):
  - **Amati geni**: no fire or light (no electric lighting visible).
  - **Amati karya**: no work.
  - **Amati lelungan**: no travel.
  - **Amati lelanguan**: no entertainment.
- **What is closed**: Ngurah Rai airport closes for 24 hours (no flights). Streets and beaches are closed to everyone including tourists. Shops, restaurants, ATMs closed. Mobile data has been switched off island-wide in several recent years; fixed wifi in accommodation generally stays on **(exact scope varies by year)**. Hospitals and emergency services run.
- Visitors must stay inside their accommodation, keep lights low and curtains shut, keep noise down.
- **Pecalang** are the only people on the streets, patrolling to see the rules are kept.
- The night sky is exceptionally dark and full of stars **(unverified, universally reported)**.
- **Ngembak Geni**, the day after: "relighting the fire"; families visit and ask each other's forgiveness.

### Odalan (temple anniversary)
- Each temple celebrates its founding anniversary every 210 days (some follow the Saka calendar and are yearly **(unverified)**).
- Lasts one day for a small temple, about three days typically, up to eleven for major ones.
- The deities are invited to descend and be honoured with offerings, music and dance.
- What a neighbour sees: the temple dressed in yellow and white cloth and umbrellas, penjor at the gate, women carrying tall fruit-and-cake offerings (**gebogan**) on their heads **(term unverified)**, gamelan late into the night, food carts outside, a road half-closed, pecalang directing scooters.
- With three village temples plus family, clan and subak temples, an odalan near any given house is frequent.

### Melasti
- A purification procession to the sea (or a spring or lake): sacred objects (**pratima**), umbrellas, banners and gamelan, the whole village in white.
- Largest before Nyepi; also held before major temple festivals.
- On Canggu's beaches this means hundreds of people in white crossing the sand past surfboards. Surfers and beach clubs are expected to give way.

### Purnama, Tilem, Kajeng Kliwon
- **Purnama (full moon)**: auspicious day of prayer and gratitude; more offerings, temple visits, many ceremonies scheduled on it.
- **Tilem (new moon)**: quieter, associated with reflection and cleansing; prayer usually later in the day.
- **Kajeng Kliwon** (every 15 days): a day when lower spirits are considered active; extra segehan are placed at gates and crossroads for protection.

### Ngaben (cremation)
- The cremation releases the soul from the body so it can continue its journey; it is a duty of the family and banjar, and the tone of the procession is energetic, not hushed mourning.
- Objects: the **bade / wadah**, a tall tiered tower that carries the body and models the cosmos; the **lembu**, a bull-shaped sarcophagus in which the body is burned. Which families use which form, and how many tiers, depends on descent group **(sources differ)**.
- The tower is carried on a bamboo grid by dozens of men and **turned at crossroads** so the soul cannot find its way back home.
- Because it is expensive, many villages hold a **mass cremation (ngaben massal)** every few years; bodies are buried in the interim **(unverified)**.
- Visitor etiquette: traffic stops; do not honk or push through; step aside; photos of the procession are generally tolerated at a distance, but not close-ups of the body or the fire without the family's permission.

### Road closures
- Processions, odalan, cremations and ogoh-ogoh routinely close or narrow roads. Pecalang direct traffic; drivers wait. This is normal and not an inconvenience to be "solved". In a game it is a good, honest reason for a route to be temporarily blocked.

### What is appropriate to depict, and how
| Ceremony | Depict? | How |
|---|---|---|

| Penjor during Galungan | Yes, ambient | Seasonal street dressing with a one-line explanation |
| Galungan / Kuningan atmosphere | Yes, ambient | Families in temple dress, closed shops, visiting day |
| Odalan | Yes, from outside | Player can watch from the outer yard or road; inner court not enterable |
| Melasti | Yes, at a distance | Procession crosses the beach; player is stopped and waits |
| Ogoh-ogoh parade | Yes, carefully | Spectator event; use original designs, do not copy a real banjar's ogoh-ogoh; no "fight the demon" mechanic |

## 3. Community structure

### Banjar
- The **banjar** is the basic unit of village self-government: a neighbourhood association of households. Two overlapping forms: **banjar adat** (customary and religious affairs) and **banjar dinas** (state administration).
- Several banjar make up a **desa adat** (customary village), led by a **bendesa adat**. Each banjar is led by an elected **kelian banjar**.
- Rules are written in the village's **awig-awig** (customary law) and supplementary **perarem**; banjar rules may not contradict the desa adat's awig-awig.
- Members (**krama**) are households, traditionally represented by the married male head. Obligations: attend meetings, contribute labour and money, take part in each other's ceremonies, especially cremations.
- **Ngayah**: unpaid devotional work for temple and community (building, cooking, decorating, playing gamelan).
- The **bale banjar** is the open meeting pavilion: meetings, gamelan and dance practice, ogoh-ogoh building, ceremony preparation, sometimes a kindergarten, badminton or a co-op kiosk **(secondary uses unverified)**.
- Non-Balinese residents usually pay a contribution to the banjar and are expected to follow its rules on ceremonies and noise **(unverified, widely reported)**.

### Kulkul
- A slit drum made from a hollowed log, hung in a raised tower (**bale kulkul**) at the banjar and the temple.
- Different beat patterns carry different messages: slow beats to call members to ngayah, other patterns for a death, a meeting, or an emergency. Awig-awig specify that only authorised people may strike it.
- Game rule: the player should never be able to hit the kulkul.

### Pecalang
- Traditional village security, attached to the desa adat; they answer to village leadership, not the police, and work alongside police and Satpol PP.
- Duties: traffic and crowd control at ceremonies, guarding processions, enforcing Nyepi. About 22,000 were deployed for Nyepi in 2017.
- **Dress**: udeng headcloth, a black vest over a shirt, a **black-and-white checked (poleng) sarong/saput**. Often a keris tucked at the back and a walkie-talkie or light baton today; vests often carry the word "PECALANG" and the village name **(keris, radio and lettering unverified)**. Some villages use red-black-white poleng **(unverified)**.
- They are unpaid volunteers doing ngayah **(unverified)**.
- Tone: calm, respected neighbours, not police and not comic relief.

### Gotong royong
- The Indonesia-wide term for mutual cooperation / communal work. In Bali it is expressed through the banjar and subak: cleaning the neighbourhood, repairing the temple, preparing a neighbour's ceremony, clearing irrigation channels.
- Beach and river clean-ups are a current, visible form in Canggu **(unverified)**.

### Subak
- A subak is a farmers' cooperative that shares and manages irrigation water from one source through canals, tunnels and weirs. It is democratic and self-governing, and it is tied to a network of water temples.
- UNESCO inscribed the **Cultural Landscape of Bali Province: the Subak System as a Manifestation of the Tri Hita Karana Philosophy** in **2012**, under criteria (iii), (v) and (vi). The system dates back to about the 9th century **(date unverified in this pass)**.
- The listed property has five components (about 19,500 ha): Pura Ulun Danu Batur, Lake Batur, the Pekerisan watershed subaks, the Catur Angga Batukaru subaks (which include Jatiluwih), and Pura Taman Ayun. **Canggu's rice fields are not part of the listed area**, though they are run by subaks in the same way.
- Water is treated as a gift of **Dewi Danu** (goddess of the crater lake); rice is the gift of **Dewi Sri** (goddess of rice and fertility).
- Each subak has a head (**pekaseh**) **(term unverified)** and its own temple; members meet, set planting dates together, and hold a ritual at every stage of the crop.
- Why coordinated planting matters: neighbouring subaks flooding and fallowing at the same time breaks pest cycles and shares scarce water (the research of anthropologist J. Stephen Lansing).
- UNESCO itself has recorded that the system is highly vulnerable; land conversion to housing and tourism is the main threat. In Canggu this is visible street by street: paddies next to villas and cafes. A game about Canggu can show this honestly without preaching.

### The rice cycle and how fields look
Most varieties take around five months seed to harvest **(sources differ: one says traditional varieties took about seven)**; with irrigation, two crops and sometimes three a year **(unverified)**. Rituals mark each stage, starting with **Mapag Toya** ("welcoming the water") at the subak temple.

| Stage | What the field looks like | People and details |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Flooding and ploughing | Brown mud, then a still mirror of water reflecting sky and palms | Stubble soaked; ploughed by hand tractor today, formerly by cattle; bunds (*pematang*) repaired with mud |
| 2. Seedbed | One small corner of bright, dense yellow-green | Seedlings raised in a walled-off patch for a few weeks |
| 3. Transplanting | Mirror water with neat rows of thin green shoots | Planted by hand, shoot by shoot, walking backwards; traditionally men's work |
| 4. Growing | Solid vivid green carpet, water hidden; alternately flooded and drained | Weeding; small offerings on bamboo field shrines; ducks herded through to eat pests and fertilise |
| 5. Flowering / grain filling | Taller, darker green with pale heads; the rice is described as "pregnant" and honoured with a ceremony (*biukukung*) **(unverified)** | Field shrine to Dewi Sri dressed |
| 6. Ripening | Gold, heads bent over, fields drained dry | Bird-scaring: strings of flags, tins and plastic strips, bamboo clappers, scarecrows (*lelakut*) **(term unverified)** |
| 7. Harvest | Patchwork of gold and cut stubble | Everyone takes part; cut with a sickle today (traditionally a small palm knife, *ani-ani*, for older varieties **(unverified)**); threshed in the field by beating bundles against a board |
| 8. After harvest | Stubble, smoke, then brown again | Stalks burned (smoke haze in late afternoon); duck flocks led in by a herder with a flag on a pole to glean fallen grain; egrets (white herons) follow the plough |

Other field details:
- Small bamboo or stone shrines at the water inlet of each plot.
- Coconut palms and banana on the bunds; narrow concrete irrigation channels with sluice gates along the lanes.
- Bamboo wind instruments (*sunari*) and wind-driven clappers (*pindekan*) in fields **(unverified)**.

## 4. Dress and etiquette

### Temple dress (pakaian adat)
- **Sarong / kamen**: a cloth wrapped around the waist covering the legs to below the knee. Required for everyone, any gender, to enter a temple.
- **Sash (selendang / senteng)**: tied round the waist over the sarong; said to bind the lower, less pure part of the body from the upper **(meaning unverified)**.
- Shoulders covered. Major temples lend or rent sarong and sash at the gate.
- **Men**: **udeng** (folded headcloth with a knot at the front), a shirt (often white for prayer), **kamen**, and an outer hip cloth, the **saput**. The udeng's central knot is said to stand for focused thought **(unverified)**.
- **Women**: **kebaya** (fitted, often lace, blouse), kamen, and sash tied over the kebaya at the waist; hair tied back, often with a frangipani flower.
- **Colour**: white and yellow dominate for prayer; dark colours for cremations **(unverified)**.
- After praying, people often have grains of blessed rice (**bija**) on the forehead and a flower behind the ear **(unverified)**.
- Schools and government offices wear traditional dress on set days (Thursdays, Purnama, Tilem) under a 2018 governor's regulation **(unverified)**.

### Official rules for foreign visitors
Source chain: Governor's Circular Letter **No. 4 of 2023** (the original do/don't list), updated by Circular Letter **No. 7 of 2025**, issued by Governor Wayan Koster on **24 March 2025**. Summaries differ slightly in wording between the Love Bali government page and press reports; both are merged below.

Obligations (do):
1. Respect the sanctity of temples, sacred statues (*pratima*) and religious symbols.
2. Respect customs, traditions, arts and local wisdom, especially during ceremonies.
3. Dress modestly and appropriately at sacred sites, attractions and in public.
4. Behave politely at sacred sites, on roads, in restaurants and shops.
5. Pay the foreign tourist levy.
6. Use licensed guides when visiting attractions.
7. Change money only at authorised money changers; pay with Indonesian rupiah and the national QR standard (QRIS).
8. Obey traffic law: valid national or international licence, helmet, proper clothing, no overloading, no riding under the influence.
9. Use legal, licensed transport and stay in licensed accommodation.

Prohibitions (don't):
1. Enter the inner zones of a temple (utama and madya mandala) unless there to pray in Balinese traditional dress. The circular also bars people who are menstruating.
2. Climb sacred trees or structures.
3. Behave disrespectfully in sacred places, including indecent or nude photos.
4. Step on, damage or interfere with offerings and ceremonial items.
5. Litter or pollute lakes, springs, rivers, the sea and public places.
6. Use single-use plastics (bags, polystyrene, straws, plastic-packaged drinks).
7. Use abusive language, behave aggressively toward officials, locals or other visitors, or spread hate speech and hoaxes online.
8. Work or run a business without proper permits.
9. Take part in illegal activity (drugs, trade in protected flora/fauna or sacred artefacts).
10. Bargain aggressively or rudely (listed on the Love Bali page).

Enforcement: Satpol PP (civil service police) and Bali Police; residents can report misbehaviour through a published WhatsApp hotline.

### The tourist levy
- **IDR 150,000** per foreign visitor, per entry, in force since **14 February 2024** **(exact day unverified; "February 2024" confirmed)**. Children pay too; Indonesian citizens are exempt.
- Paid through the official **Love Bali** website or app (card, bank transfer, QRIS); a QR voucher is issued. Lookalike third-party sites overcharge.
- Stated purpose: protecting Balinese culture and the environment.
- Compliance has been low: officially about a third of foreign arrivals paid in 2025; a mid-2026 report put it at around 43%. Reports of stricter spot checks in 2026 **(unverified)**.
- Game relevance: an Indian founder arriving on a foreign passport pays this. A one-line arrival beat ("Levy paid. It goes to culture and environment.") is accurate.

### Everyday manners
Confirmed by the official list or multiple sources:
- Never step on offerings; walk around processions; wait when a road is closed.
- Cover up away from the beach (no shirtless or bikini riding or shopping).
- No climbing on shrines, statues, walls or sacred trees for photos.

Widely taught but **(unverified in this pass)**:
- The head is the most sacred part of the body: do not touch people's heads, including children's.
- Feet are the lowest: do not point your feet at people or shrines, and do not sit higher than a priest or the offerings.
- Give and receive with the right hand (the left is for washing); a respectful form is the right hand offered with the left touching the right forearm.
- Do not point with the index finger; use the thumb or the whole hand.
- Do not walk in front of people who are praying, and do not use flash.
- Remove shoes before entering a home.
- Public anger is poorly regarded; a calm voice and a smile get further.

## 5. Architecture and objects

| Object | What it is | Meaning in one line |
|---|---|---|
| **Candi bentar** | A tower gate split cleanly down the middle with a gap to walk through, no roof | Entrance to the outer zone of a temple (also palaces, public buildings); commonly explained as a mountain split in two, the two halves balancing each other **(explanation unverified)** |
| **Kori agung / paduraksa** | A roofed tower gate with a narrow door, often with a carved guardian face (*Bhoma*) above **(Bhoma unverified)** | Marks the passage from the middle to the innermost temple zone; the central door is for deities and priests, with side doors for people **(unverified)** |
| **Angkul-angkul** | The narrow roofed gate of a house compound | The single entrance to a family's walled world |
| **Aling-aling** | A short free-standing wall just inside a gate | Blocks the direct view in, and blocks negative influences, which are believed to travel only in straight lines |
| **Bale** | An open-sided pavilion on a raised base with a pitched roof on posts | The basic building unit of Balinese architecture; a compound is several bale, each with its own purpose |
| **Bale banjar** | The neighbourhood's open meeting hall | Where the community decides, rehearses, builds and prepares together |
| **Wantilan** | A large open pavilion with a two- or three-tiered roof | Community performances and gatherings **(cockfight association unverified; avoid depicting gambling)** |
| **Bale bengong** | A small open gazebo, usually four posts and a thatched roof, in a garden or by the fields | A place to sit, rest and daydream; "bengong" means to gaze off idly **(unverified)** |
| **Bale kulkul** | A raised tower holding the slit drum | The community's voice: calls to work, to meet, or to alarm |
| **Padmasana** | A tall stone throne with an empty seat | The seat for the supreme god (Sang Hyang Widhi); empty because the divine has no form **(reasoning unverified)** |
| **Meru** | A shrine with a stack of thatched roofs | Represents the cosmic mountain; more tiers for a higher deity **(unverified)** |
| **Alang-alang thatch** | Thick roof thatch of dried alang-alang grass | Traditional roofing of bale and shrines; shrine roofs often use black sugar-palm fibre (*ijuk*) instead **(ijuk unverified)** |
| **Paras stone** | Soft grey volcanic tuff, carved into gates, walls and statues | Easy to carve, weathers quickly and grows moss, so temples look ancient within years and are regularly re-carved **(unverified)** |
| **Red brick with paras trim** | The orange-and-grey look of south Bali (Badung) temples and gates | Regional style, good for making Canggu look like Canggu and not generic "Bali" **(unverified)** |
| **Guardian statues (dwarapala)** | Paired stone figures with clubs flanking a gate | They guard the threshold; they are dressed, given offerings and treated as inhabited |
| **Saput poleng** | Black-and-white checked cloth wrapped around statues, shrines, big trees, rocks, kulkul; worn by pecalang | Rwa Bhineda: opposites in balance, equal squares of each. It also marks the thing as spiritually inhabited or protected. Variants add grey (transition) or red |
| **Yellow and white cloth** | Wrapped on shrines and temple structures for ceremonies | Marks the shrine as dressed for the gods; purity and holiness **(meaning unverified)** |
| **Tedung** | A fringed ceremonial umbrella on a long pole | Shade and honour for a deity or sacred object; marks where the sacred presence sits. White/yellow most common; also poleng |
| **Umbul-umbul and lelontek** | Tall tapering flags that ripple in the wind; long narrow banners | Carried in processions and planted at temples in festival time **(unverified)** |
| **Kulkul** | Slit log drum | Signal instrument; only struck on authority |
| **Penjor** | Decorated curved bamboo pole | Gratitude and prosperity; Mount Agung / the naga |
| **Banyan tree (beringin)** | Huge tree with aerial roots, usually by a temple, market or crossroads, wrapped in poleng | Considered inhabited and sacred; never climbed |
| **Frangipani (jepun)** | Small tree with white-yellow flowers in every temple yard | Its flowers are used in offerings and worn behind the ear |
| **Jukung** | A narrow wooden outrigger fishing canoe with two bamboo floats and a triangular sail or small engine | Working boat of the coast; painted in bright colours |
| **Jukung eyes and prow** | Bulging painted eyes and a carved prow, often with an open mouth | The prow figure is the **gajah mina** (elephant-headed fish); the eyes ward off harm and help the boat find its way home, including at night |

Notes for Canggu specifically:
- Jukung are drawn up on the sand at several west-coast beaches; Canggu-area beaches are black volcanic sand **(unverified)**.
- Beach shrines and temple walls sit directly behind surf breaks.

## 6. Food and small trade

### How a warung works
- A **warung** is a small family-run eatery or stall, often the front room of the family's house.
- Common formats:
  - **Nasi campur display case**: a glass cabinet of pre-cooked dishes. You get a scoop of rice and point at what you want; price is added up by item. Food is cooked in the morning and served at room temperature until it runs out, so go early.
  - **Cook-to-order** warungs with a short menu (nasi goreng, mie goreng, soto, cap cay).
  - **Specialist** warungs selling one thing (babi guling, bakso, sate), which close when sold out, often by early afternoon.
  - **Kaki lima** carts (bakso, martabak, gorengan) and scooter vendors.
- Order at the counter, sit on a bench or plastic stool, pay at the end, cash or **QRIS** code. Takeaway is "bungkus", wrapped in brown paper or banana leaf **(unverified details)**.
- Sambal and kerupuk (crackers) on the table; water or tea as the default drink.
- A canang sits on the counter or at the door.
- **Halal note**: Balinese food uses a lot of pork. Many warungs are Javanese or Muslim-run and halal ("Warung Muslim", "Warung Jawa", "Masakan Padang"). An Indian vegetarian protagonist can truthfully ask for "tanpa daging" (no meat) and eat tempe, tahu, sayur and gado-gado **(phrase unverified)**.

### Dishes
- **Nasi campur (Bali)**: rice with small portions of several sides: shredded chicken, sate lilit, lawar, egg, tempe, long-bean urap, peanuts, sambal.
- **Babi guling**: spit-roast suckling pig stuffed with *base genep* spice paste; served with rice, crackling, lawar, sausage and broth. Originally ceremonial food.
- **Sate lilit**: minced fish (or pork/chicken) mixed with coconut and spice, pressed around a lemongrass or flat bamboo stick and grilled.
- **Lawar**: finely chopped vegetables, grated coconut, minced meat and spices; the red version includes fresh blood. Made communally by men for ceremonies.
- **Bubur**: rice porridge. **Bubur ayam** (savoury, chicken) or Balinese **bubur mengguh / bubur Bali** with urap for breakfast; **bubur sumsum** and **bubur injin** (black rice with coconut milk) as sweets **(unverified)**.
- **Jaje / jaja Bali**: traditional sweets of rice flour, palm sugar and coconut: *klepon* (green balls with liquid palm sugar), *laklak* (small green pancakes), *dadar gulung*, *pisang rai*. Sold at morning markets and also made for offerings.
- **Ayam betutu** and **bebek betutu**: chicken or duck slow-cooked in spice paste **(unverified)**.
- **Tipat cantok**: rice cakes and vegetables in peanut sauce, a cheap Balinese staple **(unverified)**.
- **Nasi jinggo**: a fist-sized banana-leaf packet of rice with a little chicken, noodles and sambal, sold at night from scooters and stalls **(unverified)**.
- **Bakso**: meatball soup from a pushcart; the seller taps a bowl or wooden block to announce himself **(unverified)**.
- **Sambal matah**: raw shallot, lemongrass and chilli relish in coconut oil.

### Morning market (pasar pagi)
- Starts before dawn, around 4:00 to 5:00, and is mostly over by 9:00 to 10:00 **(unverified)**.
- Sections: vegetables, fruit, fish, chicken and pork, spices; a big section for **offering materials**: flowers by the bag (marigold, frangipani, hydrangea, impatiens), janur, pandan, ready-made canang, incense, bamboo trays.
- Jaje stalls and breakfast porridge sellers; women carrying loads on their heads; bargaining is normal but gentle.
- A small market temple (**pura melanting**) where traders place offerings for honest trade **(unverified)**.
- Canggu area has local markets plus tourist-facing weekend markets (Old Man's, Samadi, La Brisa) that are a different thing entirely **(unverified)**.

### Drinks
- **Kopi Bali / kopi tubruk**: coarse-ground coffee with boiling water poured straight on, served in a glass, usually sweet; wait for the grounds to settle and do not drink the last centimetre **(unverified)**.
- **Es kelapa muda**: young coconut, either whole with a straw and spoon, or the water and flesh in a glass with ice and syrup.
- **Jamu**: traditional herbal tonic; the common one is **kunyit asam** (turmeric and tamarind, yellow-orange). Traditionally sold by a *jamu gendong* woman carrying bottles in a basket on her back **(unverified; this is Javanese in origin)**. Balinese herbal drink is *loloh*, e.g. green **loloh cemcem** **(unverified)**.
- **Es teh / teh botol**, **es jeruk** (iced orange), **es daluman** (green grass jelly with coconut milk and palm sugar) **(daluman unverified)**.
- **Arak** and **tuak** (palm spirits) exist and arak has protected heritage status under a 2020 governor's regulation **(unverified)**. Recommendation: leave alcohol out or keep it background only.

## 7. Language flavour

Two languages are in daily use. **Bahasa Indonesia** is the national language and the safe default with everyone. **Basa Bali** (Balinese) is the home and ceremony language, and has speech levels (polite/high versus everyday/low); a foreigner using a little polite Balinese is warmly received. Many service workers in Canggu are from Java, Lombok, Sumba or Flores and are not Balinese, so Indonesian is the respectful default until you know.

### Balinese names by birth order
- Most Balinese (the commoner majority, often called Sudra/jaba) are named by birth order, the same names for any gender:
  - **1st**: Wayan, Putu, Gede (male); **Ni Luh** / Luh for a first-born girl.
  - **2nd**: Made, Kadek, Nengah.
  - **3rd**: Nyoman, Komang.
  - **4th**: Ketut.
  - **5th**: the cycle restarts; often "Wayan Balik" (Wayan again).
- Gender marker in front: **I** for men, **Ni** for women. E.g. *I Wayan ...*, *Ni Ketut ...*, followed by a personal name.
- Everyday short forms: Yan, De, Dek, Man, Mang, Tut **(some unverified)**.
- Descent-group titles appear in other families: **Ida Bagus / Ida Ayu** (Brahmana), **Anak Agung, Cokorda / Tjokorda, I Gusti, Dewa / Desak** (Ksatria), and others. Someone who marries into a higher group may take the title **Jero**.
- People are asked their birth-order name and called by it; so a street has many Wayans and Mades, and nicknames or the personal name tell them apart.
- For game NPCs: use birth-order plus a personal name ("Bu Kadek Sari", "Pak Wayan Suta", "Bli Komang"), and avoid inventing high-title characters casually.
- Smaller families mean Nyoman and Ketut are becoming rarer; this has been publicly discussed in Bali **(unverified)**.

## 8. Do / don't depict (summary rules; the full list is at the end)

Principles:
1. **Sacred things are scenery with meaning, never mechanics.** If it is prayed to, prayed with, or prayed in, the player cannot collect it, score with it, break it, climb it or wear it as a costume.
2. **The player is a guest who is learning.** Interactions are watching, waiting, greeting, asking, buying, helping when invited.
3. **Balinese people are residents with jobs, families and opinions**, not mystical guides or servers. Give them names, routines and lines that are not about the player.
4. **Explain once, briefly, in the voice of a neighbour**, not a museum label.
5. **Say what the game simplified.** A compressed Canggu is a fiction; credit the real thing.

## Open questions and caveats

- **Canggu specifics not found**: names and boundaries of the desa adat and banjars in the Canggu / Berawa / Pererenan area, names of local subaks, and local odalan dates. Do not invent real-sounding banjar or temple names; either research them on the ground or use clearly fictional ones.
- **Nyepi 2027 date conflict**: 8 March versus 9 March. Check the official Indonesian joint ministerial holiday decree (SKB) before hard-coding.
- **Mobile data on Nyepi** has changed year to year. Depict as "the signal goes quiet" without claiming a universal rule.
- **Prices** move fast in Canggu. Treat them as flavour, keep them in one config file.
- **The menstruation rule** in the official circular is real and contested. Recommendation: do not surface it in the game; the player simply does not enter inner temple courts.
- **Cremation objects and caste**: details vary by family and descent group and sources conflict. Avoid caste commentary entirely.
- **A Balinese cultural reviewer** (ideally someone from the Canggu area, or a cultural body such as a local desa adat or an academic at ISI Denpasar / Universitas Udayana) should read the in-game text before release. This research cannot substitute for that.
- **The protagonist is Indian and likely Hindu-background.** Balinese Hinduism shares gods and epics with Indian Hinduism but differs substantially in practice (offerings, ancestor focus, calendars, temple form, no cow taboo on beef for many **(unverified)**, pork is central). The honest and interesting stance is "familiar words, unfamiliar practice", not "we are the same". Note that "Namaste" is not the Balinese greeting; "Om Swastiastu" is.

## Most game-usable details

Ambient scenes:
1. **The morning round.** 6:30: a woman in sarong and sash walks her shopfront with a tray, sets a canang on the step, the counter and her scooter seat, flicks holy water with a flower, leaves an incense stick smoking. Repeat at every doorway on the street with different people.
2. **Offerings age through the day.** Bright at 7:00, sun-faded by noon, flattened by evening, swept up with a palm-rib broom. A cheap, truthful way to show time passing.
3. **Rice fields as a slow clock.** Cycle each field through mirror water, thin green rows, deep green, gold, stubble with smoke, and stagger neighbouring plots only slightly (a subak plants together).
4. **The duck parade.** After harvest a herder leads a flock into the stubble with a flag on a pole; the ducks stay near the flag. White egrets follow the plough.
5. **Villa beside paddy.** A cafe wall directly against a working field with a bamboo field shrine. The real tension of Canggu, shown without comment.
6. **Bale banjar at dusk.** Gamelan rehearsal, kids on the steps, men talking, and in the weeks before Nyepi a half-built ogoh-ogoh under a tarpaulin.
7. **Galungan streets.** For about five weeks, penjor arch over the lane from every gate; on the day itself shops are shut and families ride past three to a scooter in white and yellow.
8. **Nyepi.** One in-game day with no traffic, no lights, no music, pecalang on the empty road, and the Milky Way. The player stays in the compound. Do not add a task.
9. **Melasti on the surf beach.** A white-clad procession with umbrellas and gamelan crosses the sand to the water; surfers wait at the edge.
10. **Road closed for ceremony.** A pecalang with a raised hand, a line of idling scooters, nobody honking. Two minutes, then the road reopens.
11. **Sea temple behind the break.** Pura Batu Bolong's walls and tiered shrines visible from the lineup, umbrellas up on festival days.
12. **Jukung on black sand at dawn.** Painted outriggers with bulging eyes pulled above the tide line; fishermen sorting the catch.

Props with meaning:
13. **Canang sari**: a daily thank-you to the divine in a palm-leaf tray; never stepped on.
14. **Segehan on the ground at a gate**: an offering to the lower spirits to keep things in balance.
15. **Poleng cloth on a statue or banyan**: black and white in equal squares, opposites in balance; marks the thing as inhabited and protected.
16. **Penjor**: curved bamboo pole of gratitude for the earth's prosperity; the arch recalls Mount Agung.
17. **Aling-aling**: the little wall behind a gate; bad influences only travel straight, so they cannot get in.
18. **Candi bentar**: the split gate into a temple's outer yard; you pass between two halves of one whole.
19. **Kulkul tower**: the banjar's voice; different rhythms call a meeting, communal work, or an alarm.
20. **Tedung umbrella**: shade and honour for a deity; where you see one, something sacred is present.
21. **Gajah mina prow and painted eyes on a jukung**: the elephant-fish that guards the crew and sees the way home.
22. **House shrine in the kaja-kangin corner**: where a family honours its ancestors every day.

Respectful interactions a player could have:
23. **Greet properly.** "Om Swastiastu" with hands together to a Balinese elder; "Selamat pagi, Bu" at the warung; NPCs answer warmly and sometimes teach the reply ("Suksma" / "Suksma mewali").
24. **Eat at a warung.** Point at dishes in the glass case, pay about IDR 25,000 by QRIS, hear "hati-hati" on the way out. A vegetarian order is possible.
25. **Wait.** Being stopped by a ceremony and choosing to switch off the engine and watch is the correct, rewarded behaviour (rewarded with a line of dialogue or a journal note, not points).
26. **Borrow a sarong and sash** at a temple gate and stand in the outer yard; the inner gate is simply closed to the player, with a kind one-line reason.
27. **Ask a neighbour.** One tap on a prop opens a short explanation in an NPC's voice ("My mother makes forty of these before breakfast").
28. **Help when asked.** A banjar neighbour invites the player to help carry bamboo or sweep the lane on a gotong royong morning. Helping with the offerings themselves is not offered.
29. **Walk around.** Pathing and physics make the player route around offerings on the pavement; a scooter cannot ride over one.
30. **Learn names.** Meeting two Wayans and three Mades and learning what the names mean is a genuine first-month experience in Bali.

## Do / don't list for a respectful game

### Never
- **No collecting, scoring, trading or crafting with sacred objects.** Canang, incense, holy water, pratima, tedung, poleng cloth and penjor are never pickups, currency, loot, ingredients or power-ups.
- **No destroying or disturbing them.** Offerings, shrines, statues, penjor and temple walls are not physics toys. Vehicles and the player cannot crush, kick, knock over, graffiti or climb them. Do not even include the animation.
- **No ceremony as a mini-game.** No rhythm game on the gamelan in a temple, no "carry the offering without dropping it" challenge, no ogoh-ogoh boss fight, no timed cremation procession, no "complete the ritual" quest.
- **No playable priest, no player-performed rituals, no praying for stat buffs.** The player does not make offerings to gain luck, health or money.
- **No entering inner temple courts.** Outer yard at most, in sarong and sash. Pura Dalem, cemeteries and cremation grounds are not explorable spaces.
- **No cremation as spectacle.** If ngaben appears at all, it is a distant, non-interactive passing procession: no body, no fire, no close camera, no reward. Leaving it out is fine.
- **No jokes at the expense of belief.** Nothing that plays spirits, trance, "black magic", offerings or priests for laughs or for horror. No "cursed" items. No monster enemies based on Rangda, Barong, leyak or bhuta kala.
- **No sacred imagery as decoration on profane things.** Not on a surfboard, bar sign, bikini, floor texture, toilet door, loading spinner or merch. Do not use Om, a padmasana or a deity's face as a UI icon.
- **No sacred dress as a costume unlock.** Temple dress is worn in context (going to a ceremony when invited), not as a skin to surf or party in.
- **No striking the kulkul, no sitting on shrines, no touching NPC heads, no nude or lewd emotes near sacred places.**
- **No copying real, identifiable sacred things without permission.** Do not scan or replicate a specific temple's inner shrines, a specific banjar's ogoh-ogoh, or a specific family's compound. Build originals in the correct style.
- **No inventing rules or meanings and presenting them as fact.** If a meaning is uncertain, say "many people say" or leave it out.
- **No Balinese people as mystical props.** No all-knowing shaman, no "simple happy villager", no servant-only roles, no broken-English comedy.
- **No caste commentary, no depiction of the menstruation rule, no gambling/cockfight content, no drugs.** These are real and sensitive; a portfolio game is the wrong place to handle them.
- **No rewarding the badly behaved tourist.** If rude tourists appear as NPCs, the world reacts as it really does (a pecalang, a quiet word), and the player is never incentivised to join in.

### Fine as ambient life
- People placing offerings, sweeping, praying at a shopfront shrine, riding to temple in adat dress.
- Incense smoke, gamelan heard from a bale banjar, the Tri Sandhya from a loudspeaker.
- Penjor, tedung, poleng-wrapped statues and banyans, temple gates and walls seen from the street.
- Rice farmers, ducks, egrets, irrigation channels, field shrines, the full crop cycle.
- Pecalang directing traffic; a road closed for a ceremony; a procession passing.
- Ogoh-ogoh being built and paraded (original designs), watched from the roadside.
- Nyepi as a silent, dark, starry day.
- Warungs, markets, jamu and bakso sellers, jukung fishermen, kids flying kites in the dry-season wind (roughly June to September **(unverified)**).
- The mix that is actually Canggu: Balinese families, Javanese and Sumbanese workers, surfers, remote workers, construction, traffic.

### How to credit and explain
- **Explain in a neighbour's voice, one or two lines, on demand.** Tap a prop, hear a named local say what it means to them. Never a pop-up quiz.
- **Keep a "What you saw" journal** that fills in as the player notices things, using the Balinese term first, then a plain gloss.
- **Use the real words** (canang sari, penjor, banjar, subak, pecalang) with correct spelling, and do not anglicise them into "offering basket" or "village cop".
- **An "About this world" page** that states: this is a compressed, fictional Canggu; real Canggu is a set of living villages with their own banjars, temples and subaks; ceremonies shown are simplified; errors are the author's; and that the author is a guest there.
- **Name the people who checked it.** Have at least one Balinese reader review all cultural text and visuals, pay them, and credit them by name with their consent.
- **Link out** to real sources (the Love Bali visitor guidance, UNESCO's subak page) and, if the game ever earns money, consider pointing players to a local cause (a subak or banjar initiative, a river clean-up group).
- **State the author's position once.** An Indian founder moving to Bali is a newcomer with some shared vocabulary and a lot to learn. That honest framing is the game's best protection against exoticising.
